Insulin is on the Institute for Safe Medication Practices' (ISMP) list of high-alert medications — drugs that carry a heightened risk of significant patient harm when used in error — and it's tested consistently on the NCLEX for that reason. Getting comfortable with insulin math isn't just about passing an exam — it's genuinely one of the higher-stakes calculation skills in nursing practice.

## Insulin is measured in units, not mg or mL

This is the first thing to internalize: insulin dosing uses **units**, administered with a dedicated insulin syringe marked in unit increments — never convert an insulin dose into milligrams or milliliters, and never attempt to draw up insulin using a standard mL-marked syringe. Write "units" out in full; the abbreviation "U" is on safety organizations' do-not-use lists because it can be misread as a zero.

See [converting units](/blog/converting-units-mg-mcg-ml) for why units sit outside the metric ladder.

## What is a sliding scale?

A sliding scale is a pre-set protocol that adjusts how much rapid- or short-acting insulin a patient receives based on their current blood glucose reading, rather than a single fixed dose every time. A sliding scale lists blood glucose ranges, each paired with a corresponding number of insulin units to administer.

The ranges and unit amounts are **specific to each patient's order** and to facility protocol — they are not universal numbers to memorize. On an exam, the question will provide the scale; your job is to read it accurately.

## How to read a sliding scale correctly

1. Obtain the current blood glucose reading
2. Find which range on the scale the reading falls into — check whether the range boundaries are inclusive
3. Administer the corresponding unit amount for that range
4. Document the glucose reading and the dose given

If the reading falls outside the scale (very low or very high), the order usually directs you to follow a hypoglycemia protocol or notify the provider rather than give a dose.

## Why independent double-checks matter for insulin

Many clinical settings require a second nurse to independently verify an insulin dose before it's administered — not because any individual nurse is assumed to be careless, but because insulin's narrow margin for error means a system-level safeguard catches mistakes that could otherwise slip through. This is a well-documented patient safety practice, not an optional extra step.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Confusing units with mL when drawing up insulin
- Misreading a sliding scale range, especially under time pressure
- Failing to recheck blood glucose after administering a correction dose
