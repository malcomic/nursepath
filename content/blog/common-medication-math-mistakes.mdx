Most medication math errors aren't a conceptual misunderstanding — they're small, avoidable slips that happen under time pressure. Knowing the common failure points in advance makes them much easier to catch.

## 1. Decimal point errors

A misplaced decimal is one of the single most dangerous mistakes in dosage calculation, because it can turn a safe dose into a tenfold overdose or underdose without the number "looking" obviously wrong at a glance. **Habits that help:** always use a leading zero (0.5 mg, never .5 mg) and never a trailing zero (5 mg, never 5.0 mg). After calculating, say the answer out loud or write it with units clearly labeled, and pause to ask "does this amount make sense for this medication?"

## 2. Mixing up similar-looking units

mg vs. mcg, mL vs. L, units vs. mg — these look similar enough, especially handwritten or under time pressure, that mixing them up is a well-documented error pattern. **Habit that helps:** circle or highlight the unit in both the order and your calculation before solving, so a mismatch is visually obvious. Refresh the basics in [converting units](/blog/converting-units-mg-mcg-ml).

## 3. Forgetting to convert before calculating

Weight in lbs instead of kg, time in hours instead of minutes — plugging numbers into a formula before converting to the correct unit produces a confidently wrong answer. **Habit that helps:** write out every conversion as its own explicit step, rather than trying to do it mentally mid-calculation.

## 4. Rounding too early

Rounding an intermediate number in a multi-step problem, then continuing the calculation from the rounded figure, compounds small errors into a meaningfully wrong final answer. **Habit that helps:** carry full precision through every intermediate step, and only round the final answer, according to the rounding convention your program or facility uses. The [BSA dosing walkthrough](/blog/pediatric-dosing-body-surface-area) shows this effect with real numbers.

## 5. Skipping the sanity check

Getting a calculated answer and moving on without asking "does this number make sense?" is how an obviously-wrong answer (like a tablet count of 47, or an IV rate of 3,000 mL/hr) slips through. **Habit that helps:** before finalizing any dosage calculation, ask whether the result is a *practical, plausible* amount to actually administer.

## 6. Treating every calculation the same way

High-alert medications — insulin, heparin, and similar drugs — deserve extra scrutiny and, in real practice, an independent double-check, precisely because the consequence of an error is higher. Treating a routine oral tablet calculation and a heparin titration with the same level of caution undersells how much more careful the second one needs to be.
