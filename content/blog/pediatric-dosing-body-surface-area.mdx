Most medication dosing you'll learn is weight-based — but some medications, particularly many chemotherapy agents and a handful of other high-precision drugs, are dosed based on **body surface area (BSA)** instead. This shows up less often than straightforward mg/kg dosing, but when it does, it's worth understanding why.

## Why BSA instead of weight alone?

Body surface area tends to track physiological processes like metabolic rate and drug clearance more closely than weight alone, because it accounts for both height and weight. That matters most for drugs with a narrow gap between an effective dose and a toxic one — which is why BSA dosing is most strongly associated with oncology.

## Calculating BSA

The most commonly taught method is the **Mosteller formula**, which uses both height and weight (unlike mg/kg dosing, which uses weight only):

```
BSA (m²) = √( Height in cm × Weight in kg ÷ 3,600 )
```

Facilities may also use a BSA nomogram or a different validated formula. On an exam, use whatever formula the question provides; in practice, follow your facility's policy.

Once BSA is calculated (in m²), it's used in place of weight in the dosing formula:

```
Total dose = Dose ordered per m² × Patient's BSA in m²
```

## Worked example

*Order: 50 mg/m². Child's height is 100 cm and weight is 16 kg.*

**Step 1:** Multiply height by weight and divide — 100 × 16 = 1,600 → 1,600 ÷ 3,600 = 0.4444
**Step 2:** Take the square root — √0.4444 = 0.6667 m²
**Step 3:** Multiply by the ordered dose — 50 mg/m² × 0.6667 m² = **33.3 mg**

If you round BSA to 0.67 before step 3, you get 33.5 mg. Small differences like this matter for narrow-margin drugs, which is why you round only the final answer.

## Why this calculation carries extra weight in pediatrics

BSA dosing is used specifically in situations where the margin between an effective dose and a harmful one is especially narrow — which is exactly the kind of high-precision calculation where a nursing student needs to be completely comfortable with the method, not just vaguely familiar with it.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Using a weight-based (mg/kg) formula when the order actually specifies BSA-based dosing
- Calculation errors in the BSA formula itself, which compound into the final dose
- Using height in inches or weight in pounds in a formula built for cm and kg
- Using an outdated height or weight measurement for the BSA calculation
