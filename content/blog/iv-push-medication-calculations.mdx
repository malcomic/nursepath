IV push medications are administered directly into a vein (or IV line) over a short, specific period of time — and getting that timing right matters. Pushing a medication too quickly can cause adverse effects the ordered rate was specifically designed to prevent.

## The basic IV push rate formula

```
Rate (mL/min) = Volume to administer (mL) ÷ Time ordered (min)
```

This tells you how much volume to push per minute — but in practice, nurses often convert this further into a practical administration pace, such as "1 mL every X seconds," since pushing fluid evenly over a full minute by feel is harder than working in smaller intervals:

```
Seconds per mL = 60 ÷ Rate (mL/min)
```

## Worked example

*Order: Administer 4 mL of a medication IV push over 2 minutes.*

**Step 1:** Apply the formula — 4 mL ÷ 2 min = 2 mL/min
**Step 2:** Convert to a practical interval — 60 seconds ÷ 2 mL = 1 mL every **30 seconds**

## Why the ordered time matters so much

Unlike an IV drip running over hours, an IV push happens in a short, concentrated window — which means there's very little time to notice and correct an administration error before the full dose has been given. Many IV push medications have recommended administration times because giving them too fast is associated with adverse effects, so treat the ordered time as part of the order, not a suggestion.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Administering an IV push faster than ordered because the calculated interval feels slow
- Confusing an IV push rate calculation with a continuous infusion (gtt/min or mL/hr) calculation — they're different scenarios with different formulas (see [IV drip rate calculations](/blog/iv-drip-rate-calculations))
- Not confirming compatibility with other medications or fluids running on the same line before pushing
