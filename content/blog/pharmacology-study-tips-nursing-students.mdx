Pharmacology intimidates almost every nursing student — not because the math is impossible, but because the **volume** feels endless. The fix is not memorizing every drug name in isolation. It is learning **classes, prototypes, and safety patterns** you can transfer to new meds on exams and on the floor.

## Study by Class, Not by Alphabet

Group drugs the way the body and the exam do:

- Antihypertensives (ACEIs, ARBs, beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, diuretics)
- Antibiotics (by major class and key toxicities)
- Anticoagulants / antiplatelets
- Insulin and oral antidiabetics
- Psychotropics (SSRIs, antipsychotics, mood stabilizers)
- Cardiac glycosides, nitrates, antiarrhythmics

For each class, master:

1. **Prototype drug** (e.g., lisinopril for ACEIs)
2. **Therapeutic effect** — why we give it
3. **Priority adverse effects** — what can kill or harm
4. **Key nursing assessments** before/after administration
5. **Teaching points** patients must know

When you see a less familiar drug ending in *-pril*, you already know the ACEI story.

## High-Yield Suffix and Prefix Patterns

| Pattern | Often means | Watch for |
|---------|-------------|-----------|
| *-pril* | ACE inhibitor | Cough, hyperkalemia, angioedema |
| *-sartan* | ARB | Hyperkalemia, hypotension |
| *-olol* | Beta blocker | Bradycardia, masking hypoglycemia |
| *-dipine* | Dihydropyridine CCB | Peripheral edema, hypotension |
| *-statin* | HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor | Myopathy, liver enzymes |
| *-azole* | Antifungal (many) | Drug interactions, hepatotoxicity |
| *cef- / ceph-* | Cephalosporin | Cross-sensitivity considerations |

Patterns are shortcuts, not guarantees — always confirm with the stem.

## Build a One-Page Class Card

For every major class, create a single page (digital or paper):

- Mechanism in one sentence
- Top 3 side effects
- Top 3 nursing checks
- One “do not combine with…” interaction
- One NCLEX-style priority question you missed

Review class cards daily in short spaced sessions instead of rereading the whole chapter.

## Medication Safety Habits That Win Exam Points

Exams love safety:

- **Rights of medication** and high-alert meds (insulin, heparin, opioids)
- **Labs before dosing** (e.g., INR for warfarin, potassium for digoxin/diuretics)
- **Hold parameters** (HR/BP for beta blockers)
- **Peak / trough** timing for certain antibiotics
- **Antidotes** worth knowing (e.g., naloxone, vitamin K, protamine — as taught in your curriculum)

When stuck between two answers, ask: **Which option protects the patient first?**

<Callout title="Pharm SATA tip">
On select-all pharmacology items, evaluate each option as its own true/false. Do not hunt for a “usual number” of correct answers.
</Callout>

## Active Recall Beats Highlighting

Passive reading creates familiarity, not recall. Use:

- **Closed-book teaching:** Explain a class out loud in two minutes
- **Question banks:** 20–40 pharm questions daily with rationale review
- **Comparison charts:** e.g., heparin vs warfarin, rapid vs long-acting insulin
- **Case stems:** “Client on digoxin reports nausea and yellow vision — what next?”

## A Weekly Pharm Routine

- **Mon/Wed/Fri:** One class deep dive + 25 questions
- **Tue/Thu:** Mixed review of previous classes + antidotes/labs
- **Weekend:** Timed set (40–60 questions) and error log update

Track misses by **class** and **error type** (didn’t know adverse effect vs misread the stem).

## Connecting Pharm to Clinical Judgment

NGN and NCLEX rarely ask “What is the half-life?” More often:

- Which finding indicates toxicity?
- Which teaching point is priority?
- Which med should the nurse question?

Tie every drug class to **cues → action → monitoring**.
